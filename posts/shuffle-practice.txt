8:T1d7a,<p>I'm not good at shuffling a deck of playing cards, and wanted to practice the
riffle shuffle. There's a result that it takes 7 riffles to randomize a deck
(<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shuffling#Riffle">wikipedia</a>,
<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AxJubaijQbI">youtube</a>).  To see the full
effect of a shuffle, we need to know the order of cards in a deck before and
after. I have some tools to annotate a deck and inspect shuffles in the
<a href="https://github.com/robowager/shuffle_practice">shuffle_practice</a> repo.  The
repo has example
<a href="https://github.com/robowager/shuffle_practice/tree/main/data">data</a> which I
will refer to in this post.</p>
<h1>Deck image</h1>
<p>I started with capturing an image of all 52 cards in a deck. I laid them out on
the floor against a dark background to make further image processing tasks
easier.</p>
<p><img src="/shuffle-practice/20251008_01.jpg" alt="20251008_01"></p>
<p>These are some conventions I used for ordering a deck.</p>
<ul>
<li>With cards stacked face-down, the bottom-most card is the first, and the
top-most card is the last.</li>
<li>In a deck image, the order of cards is left-right, top-down. The first card is
the top-left, and last card is bottom-right.</li>
</ul>
<p><img src="/shuffle-practice/card_order.png" alt="card_order"></p>
<p>I only performed riffles, and captured a deck image after every single riffle.
In the <a href="https://github.com/robowager/shuffle_practice/tree/main/data/capture">example
sequence</a>
included with the repo, there are 8 images of decks, for a total of 7 riffles
between the first and last deck. The image above is of the <a href="https://github.com/robowager/shuffle_practice/blob/main/data/capture/20251008_01.jpg">first
deck</a>,
before any shuffles.</p>
<h1>Segmentation</h1>
<p>The next task was to segment a deck image into individual cards. I used
<a href="https://github.com/facebookresearch/segment-anything">segment-anything</a> for
this purpose. I worked on a cpu-only machine, so I used the smallest model
along with a scaled-down image. The model segmented cards very well, but could
also generate spurious segments.</p>
<p><img src="/shuffle-practice/all_masks.jpg" alt="all_masks"></p>
<p>I got rid of unwanted segments with some simple filtering (such as expected
card area and aspect ratio). This worked because I used a fixed set of playing
cards, and tried to capture consistent deck images. I often captured a couple
of images of the same deck, and chose one on which segmentation worked.</p>
<p><img src="/shuffle-practice/segmentation.jpg" alt="segmentation"></p>
<h1>Classification</h1>
<p>Once I had card segments, I used
<a href="https://github.com/mlfoundations/open_clip">open_clip</a> for labeling the cards.
Based on some
<a href="https://github.com/robowager/shuffle_practice/blob/main/data/clip_eval_results.csv">evaluation</a>
(see the final <code>deck</code> accuracy column in the linked csv), the (SigLIP, webli)
models stood out as having very good performance, without any prompt
engineering.  Perhaps the webli dataset includes playing cards, but I didn't
confirm.</p>
<p>Predictions were correct for cards that were clearly visible, such as the <code>3h</code>
<a href="https://github.com/robowager/shuffle_practice/blob/main/data/segmentation/20251008_01/19.jpg">card</a>
shown below. I took deck images on a phone, and cards near the edges were
consistently blurry. The SigLIP model I used often did well labeling even
these, such as the <code>6h</code>
<a href="https://github.com/robowager/shuffle_practice/blob/main/data/segmentation/20251008_01/00.jpg">card</a>.</p>
<p><img src="/shuffle-practice/classification_examples.jpg" alt="classification_examples"></p>
<p>I used the <a href="https://github.com/robowager/shuffle_practice/tree/main/data/classification">classification
output</a>
as a seed to manually
<a href="https://github.com/robowager/shuffle_practice/tree/main/data/annotation">annotate</a>
the decks.  Classification was pretty good (average accuracy 97% on the 8
example images) which simplified this step.</p>
<h1>Permutation stats</h1>
<p>With the <a href="https://github.com/robowager/shuffle_practice/tree/main/data/annotation">annotation
files</a>
in place, I could get an informal picture of shuffle quality. My example
sequence of shuffles was far from perfect.</p>
<p>I found looking at permutation matrices to be helpful. A permutation matrix is
a matrix of zeros and ones that maps a source sequence to a permutation. In all
the matrices shown, the source was the starting deck (<code>20251008_01</code>). The three
images below are the permutation matrix after 1, 2, and 3 riffles.  In other
words, they map deck 1 to 2, 3, and 4.</p>
<p><img src="/shuffle-practice/permutation_matrix_progression.png" alt="permutation_matrix_progression"></p>
<ul>
<li>After 1 riffle, the matrix has 2 clear diagonals, corresponding to
the cards in the left and right hand. It shows that I tended to drop
groups of 3-4 cards at once.</li>
<li>After 2 riffles, the matrix interestingly has 4 diagonals.</li>
<li>After 3 riffles, the pattern of doubling diagonals is less convincing.</li>
</ul>
<p>Shuffle 6, between decks 6 and 7, was so bad that it isn't even a riffle. I
tried to follow the riffle with a bridge, but fumbled, and undid most of the
riffle. Perhaps my sequence of 7 riffles is effectively only 6.</p>
<p><img src="/shuffle-practice/bad_shuffle.png" alt="bad_shuffle"></p>
<p>The overall permutation matrix, between decks 1 and 8, looks pretty scattered,
although a diagonal bias possibly still remains.</p>
<p><img src="/shuffle-practice/permutation_matrix_overall.png" alt="permutation_matrix_overall"></p>
<p>In a permuted sequence, a pair of elements is inverted if their order is
flipped, relative to the source sequence. For a deck of 52 cards, the expected
number of inversions for a uniform random permutation is <code>52 * 51 / 4 = 663</code>.
The number of inversions in my sequence of shuffles (relative to the starting
deck) is below.</p>
<pre><code>281, 452, 583, 618, 696, 740, 714
</code></pre>
<p>I don't have anything as principled as confidence intervals, but <code>714</code> seems
far enough from <code>663</code> to suggest that my riffle shuffle needed more practice.</p>
<h1>Conclusion</h1>
<p>At the start of the project, I thought I would learn about random permutation
statistics. Instead, I spent time working with zero-shot models for
segmentation and classification, which to their credit worked very well.</p>
<p>The limiting factor for more statistics was data collection effort.  In the
final state of my code and workflow, it took me more than a couple of hours to
capture, annotate, and inspect a sequence of 7 riffles, or 8 decks. I didn't
have enough data instances to look into questions like the following.</p>
<ul>
<li>A hypothesis test for uniform random shuffles, including a consideration of
alternatives and error rates. The wikipedia page on <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_permutation_statistics">Random permutation
statistics</a> is a
starting point.</li>
<li>Correlation statistics between successive shuffles, which
are not independent samples from a distribution.</li>
<li>Exploring whether 6 riffles is enough, another result mentioned on the
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shuffling#Riffle">wikipedia entry</a> on riffle
shuffles.</li>
</ul>
<p>I concluded this project once it became more fun, and practice, to shuffle in
service of an actual game of cards.</p>
